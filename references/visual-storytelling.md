# Design visible events before copy

Start with an information map: audience question, essential message, visible event, evidence, and intended viewer takeaway. Prioritize one main change per beat. Copy names, qualifies, or invites action; it should not carry every explanation. Preserve necessary UI labels, subtitles, and accessibility information.

Choose a representation because it fits the information:

| Information need | Useful visual construction | Failure to avoid |
| --- | --- | --- |
| How an action works | Real control, visible action, corresponding result | An interface reacting with no cause |
| What changes | Comparable starting and ending states | Invented performance or output |
| How things relate | Grouping, paths, containment, alignment, scale | Decorative connections with no meaning |
| Why the outcome matters | Specific context and visible use of the result | Generic lifestyle imagery replacing proof |
| An abstract idea | A physical or graphic metaphor with a clear correspondence | An attractive puzzle requiring a paragraph |

These are representation choices, not mandatory shot categories. An operation can contain several of them. A literal demo is often the clearest choice.

## Concept selection

Compare three different organizing ideas before writing full choreography. For each name the opening picture, central visible change, real product evidence, handoff, and production risk. Evaluate comprehension, truth, distinctiveness, and feasibility in short prose. Autonomous mode chooses without a stage approval; collaborative mode lets the user choose. Honor an explicitly selected template.

Derive art direction from product cues, audience, message, and distribution context. Specify framing and negative space, contrast and palette, material, spatial relations, light direction, motion character, and sound character. Avoid a menu of style labels. Maintain one consistent visual world while varying scale, density, tempo, and viewpoint for meaning.

## Storyboard and rough cut

Each beat records duration, viewer takeaway, subject, start state, trigger, visible response, evidence, camera, light, reading hold, and handoff. Identify what remains clear without promotional headlines. Do not remove essential labels merely to reduce a word count.

Use key frames to explore hierarchy, then compare actual audible treatments before extending the film; follow audiovisual-development.md. A good still can hide a poor transition. Prefer a useful cut over forced continuous motion. Review thumbnail silhouettes for hierarchy and full-size moving frames for evidence and legibility.

## Review

Ask what the viewer can infer from the picture, whether the inference is true, and whether a simpler construction works. Explicitly challenge: opaque metaphor, misleading product implication, inconsistent generated material, distracting spatial motion, and static-image animation posing as visual storytelling. Record actual defects, fixes, and timestamps. Do not claim comprehension from a pixel scanner or an aesthetic score.
